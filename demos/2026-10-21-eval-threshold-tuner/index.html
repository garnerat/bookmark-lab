Tune the judge before you trust 0.50

A judge in an eval returns a score from 0 to 1. A cutoff turns that score into a decision. 0.50 is a common default, and it is only a guess. This page lets you move the cutoff and see which mistakes you take.

The idea is from Hamel Husain’s post about the essay How To Use Decision Models for Evals by Hamel Husain and Antaripa Saha: choose the decision model on purpose, tune its threshold instead of assuming 0.50, and learn what the scores mean. In that essay the score is a probability of failure. Here the score is the chance a support draft is good enough to send. Same kind of decision, labels turned around.

Synthetic example data. 160 made-up support drafts for a fictional bike shop. The labels follow the shop rules below. The three judges were constructed for this page. Nothing here is a real customer, a live model score, or a result from the essay. The rows are in data.js.

Drafts at or above the cutoff would be sent to the customer. Drafts below it would be held for a person. Precision is the share of sent drafts that are actually good. Recall is the share of good drafts that get sent. A false pass is a bad draft that reaches the customer. A false fail is a good draft a person has to re-check.

The cost slider asks which mistake is more expensive, then marks the cutoff with the lowest total on these drafts. The reliability chart asks a different question: when the judge says 0.80, are about 80% of those drafts actually good? The calibrated judge was built to land near that line. The other two were bent on purpose. On a real eval you plot the chart to find out which kind of judge you have.

Changing this ratio does not rescore the drafts. It only moves the recommended cutoff. Cost = (bad drafts sent) × N + (good drafts held).
