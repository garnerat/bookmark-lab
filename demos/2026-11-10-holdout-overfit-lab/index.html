Westwood Air is a made-up heating shop in Westwood, Cincinnati. You are tuning a small assistant that drafts replies to customer messages. The score on the messages it studies can climb toward 100% while messages it has never seen get worse than the base assistant.

From @aaronklaw’s post on learning to fine-tune legal models. He used Unsloth’s free notebooks. The first model looked excellent on the data it had studied, then did worse than the base model on anything new. This page simulates that lesson. It is not his model.

Simulation, not a trained model. Westwood Air, the messages, and the price book are fictional. Nothing here is a quote for a real job, legal advice, or a legal model. Nothing is uploaded. The page does not call a network. Every score is a checklist graded in this tab.

What went wrong the first time

The training loss fell and the studied examples looked right. New matters did not. The same trap shows up if you grade a prompt or an agent on the examples you used to tune it.

  1. Split first. Take the hold-out out before any tuning and put it in another folder, with a note that says what it is.
  2. Write the definition first. Decide what a good reply is before you look at a score.
  3. Near zero is a warning. A small model memorizes fast. A perfect score on the tune set is a reason to look at messages it has not seen.

Takeaway

  2. Be suspicious of near-perfect scores.
